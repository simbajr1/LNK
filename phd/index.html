Conference reflections and paper highlights

Conference: 4th International Conference and Fair on Land and Development and 8th Lateef Jakande Annual Lecture, held on 24–25 November 2026 at the University of Lagos.

Where are the historical survey plans of Lagos?

Theme: Leveraging Digital Technologies for Sustainable Land Governance, Smart Urban Management, and Inclusive Housing Delivery in Africa.

As a rapporteur, I listened to fascinating presentations and left with several questions. One was about Lateef Jakande's legacy: his ambitious investment in public education and housing, including thousands of classrooms and residential estates. What stops us from building on that legacy and sustaining the development initiatives of previous generations?

I also reflected on the relationship between cadastral information, land tenure and sustainable urban development. Cadastral surveys establish boundaries, parcel dimensions, ownership interests and public utility spaces—the foundation for land-use planning, zoning, infrastructure and development control. But what happens when these records are incomplete, fragmented or inaccessible?

Lagos has a complex land administration history, where customary landholding coexists with statutory systems. Tenure insecurity, overlapping claims and competing institutional interests continue to complicate development. Yet the city's road layouts, street patterns and addressing systems visible in satellite images point to earlier planning and surveying efforts. Where are the historical survey plans and Registry Index Maps (RIMs) that informed them?

Perhaps the question is not whether Lagos has a cadastre, but whether its historical records have been preserved, reconciled, integrated and made accessible for contemporary planning. The right to the city also asks how people can belong when tenure is uncertain and displacement threatens established communities. Security is not only formal title: it includes recognizing legitimate rights, protecting livelihoods and enabling people to invest with confidence in their homes and communities.

At a public lecture on Professor Akin Mabogunje's legacy, I encountered the idea of a palimpsest: layers of writing that retain traces of earlier inscriptions. Lagos can be read in the same way—a precolonial, colonial and postcolonial city, each leaving marks on the landscape and institutions we inherit.

A smart city cannot be built on disconnected land records, insecure tenure and forgotten planning histories. Digital transformation must also mean institutional reform, historical accountability and social inclusion. Are we using technology only to make land administration more efficient, or also to make our cities more just?

Liverpool Early Career Researchers Conference

Conference theme: Coastal Urbanism in West Africa and Beyond.

Authors: Loise Kitulu, Victor Onifade and Silas Muketha.

Paper: "Towards Circular Urban Water Systems: A Comparative SEEIT Assessment of Wastewater Reuse Barriers in Lagos and Nairobi."

Presentation highlights

  • Core principle: Feasibility depends on technical, institutional, ecological and societal systems evolving together, not treatment hardware alone.
  • Lagos: Harmonize governance across LSWMO, LSWRC and I-WC; consider hybrid plants to bypass a 14-37% state budget burden; replenish aquifers through managed aquifer recharge; and build public trust through professional medical endorsement.
  • Nairobi: Establish binding quality standards under NWSS 2021-2025; address pathogen and heavy-metal contamination affecting 720+ hectares of agriculture; expand constructed wetlands and ponds; and support farmers through micro-credit and training.

Water Access Conference 2026

Paper: "Centralized and Decentralized Wastewater Management in Lagos and Nairobi: A Comparative Systematic Review."

Authors: Loise Kitulu, Timothy G. Nubi and Victor Onifade.

Paper highlights

  • A PRISMA-guided review of 38 peer-reviewed studies from Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar compared wastewater systems in Lagos and Nairobi.
  • Both cities face weak institutional coordination, limited sewerage coverage, insufficient financing and pollution from untreated wastewater. Lagos systems are fragmented, while Nairobi has more structured hybrid systems combining centralized, decentralized and nature-based approaches.
  • Sustainable hybrid systems need stronger governance, improved maintenance and regulatory compliance, behavioral interventions and decentralized innovation.
